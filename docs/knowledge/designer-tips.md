# Small tips for designers

Short reminders for each stage of the work. These are rules of thumb, so break one whenever you have a good reason.

## Discovery
- Talk to someone who does the task before you sketch it.
- Ask "Tell me about the last time you did this." Real stories beat opinions.
- Start wide, then narrow. Save your specific questions for the end.
- If your question could be answered with "yes", try rewriting it with "how" or "what".
- Don't name the button you're asking about. You've just told them it matters.
- Let silence run a few seconds longer than feels comfortable.
- Write what they said and what you think it means in separate places.

## Define
- A good problem statement names who is stuck, on what, and why it matters.
- If a persona has no research behind it, it's a guess with a stock photo.
- Agree what you're not solving. It will save a meeting later.
- Write down decisions and the reason for them. Future you will ask.

## IA and flows
- Name things the way your users do, even if your team says it differently.
- Draw the unhappy path too: wrong password, no results, lost connection.
- Every screen needs an answer to "where am I, and how do I get out?"
- Don't be clever with navigation. People learn it on other products first.

## Wireframes
- Stay in grayscale until the hierarchy works without colour.
- Use real content early. "Lorem ipsum" hides every hard problem.
- Try the longest name and the biggest number you can imagine.
- Sketch three different layouts before you polish one.
- Cover the content with your hand. Is the next action still obvious?

## Visual design
- Squint at the screen. The thing that stands out should be the thing that matters.
- One primary button per screen. If everything is loud, nothing is.
- Start with more space than you think you need, then take some away.
- Keep items in a group closer together than the groups are to each other.
- Before adding a border, try using space instead.
- Check dark mode as carefully as light mode.
- Use colour with meaning, then repeat that meaning everywhere.
- Before adding a new style or component, check whether you already have one.

## Writing
- Delete half the words, then see if you can delete a few more.
- "Delete project" beats "OK". Buttons should say what they do.
- A good error says what happened and how to fix it, without blaming anyone.
- Treat an empty screen as an invitation: what goes here and how do I start?
- Read it out loud. If you wouldn't say it, rewrite it.

## Forms
- Keep labels above fields, and visible while people type.
- Question every field. Who uses this answer, and for what?
- Never clear what someone already typed because of one mistake.
- Allow paste in password fields. Password managers help people.

## Interaction and motion
- If something takes over a second, show that it's working.
- Motion should explain a change. If it only decorates, remove it.
- Make exits slightly quicker than entrances.
- Offer undo where you can. It's kinder than "Are you sure?"

## Accessibility
- Tab through your screen without touching the mouse.
- Zoom to 200% and see what breaks.
- Try it in grayscale. Does colour carry anything that nothing else does?
- Test a real phone with your thumb, because a mouse is much more precise.
- An automated check finds some problems, but it can't tell you it's accessible.

## Devices
- Rethink for each size, don't just shrink.
- Keep the main action within thumb reach on phones.
- Don't hide anything important behind hover, because touch screens have none.

## Testing
- Five people per round usually reveals plenty. Test again after you fix things.
- Give a goal, not instructions. "Find a gift under $20", not "Use the filter".
- When someone struggles, stay quiet and watch.
- Ask "What did you expect to happen?"
- Fix what stops people first, and leave the cosmetic issues until later.

## Working with AI agents
- Be specific. "Editorial, warm, dense" gets further than "clean and modern".
- Give the agent your tokens, decisions and real content first.
- Polished isn't the same as usable. Check hierarchy, grouping and contrast yourself.
- Ask whether each generated element needs to exist. Agents love adding things.
- Ask for empty, error and loading states. They rarely come by default.
- Verify every quote and statistic an agent gives you against the source.
- Keep the decision with you. An agent can draft, but a person approves.

## Handoff
- Annotate behaviour, not just appearance.
- Include limits: longest text, empty case, error case, slowest connection.
- Walk a developer through it before calling it finished.
- Compare the built version with the design. Small differences add up.

## Reviews
- Show the problem and the goal before you show the screen.
- Ask for specific feedback: "Is the next step clear?" gets better answers than "Thoughts?"
- Respond to feedback with the reasoning, not a defence.
- After launch, look at what happened. Then decide what to change next.

## Stay honest
- Make cancelling as easy as signing up.
- Show the full price as early as you can.
- Let people say no without making them feel bad.
- If you wouldn't want it done to you, don't ship it.

## Worth reading
- *Don't Make Me Think*, Steve Krug: clear, short and the place to start
- *The Design of Everyday Things*, Don Norman: how people understand things
- *Refactoring UI*, Adam Wathan and Steve Schoger: practical visual fixes
- *Laws of UX*, Jon Yablonski: psychology you can apply
- *Form Design Patterns*, Adam Silver: forms that work for everyone
- *100 Things Every Designer Needs to Know About People*, Susan Weinschenk: a good one to dip into
- *The Non-Designer's Design Book*, Robin Williams: the basics of visual design
- [Nielsen Norman Group](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/) and [Baymard Institute](https://baymard.com/blog): ongoing research
- [WCAG 2.2](https://www.w3.org/WAI/standards-guidelines/wcag/new-in-22/): the accessibility standard
